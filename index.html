Data Analytics Case Study Guide

How Data Solves Real Business Problems

Every business, from a corner supermarket chain to a bank, a hospital or a food delivery app, makes hundreds of decisions every day. When those decisions are based on gut feeling, money is wasted and customers are lost. Data analysis replaces guessing with evidence: it shows what is really happening, why, and what to do next. In this guide you will walk through 10 real-world-style problems from 10 industries and see exactly how an analyst solves each one, step by step.

Please note: all companies, people and numbers in this guide are fictional, created only for learning. They are realistic, but they are not real company data.

FreshKart Mart: Too much stock in some stores, empty shelves in others

Regional supermarket chain, 42 stores across Tamil Nadu · Beginner

📌1Snapshot Card

Domain
Retail
Company type
Regional supermarket chain, 42 stores across Tamil Nadu
Analyst role
Retail / Inventory Analyst
Difficulty
Beginner
Tools used
Excel, SQL, Python (Pandas), Power BI
The question this case answers

Why do some stores have too much stock while others run out of the same product?

🏢2Business Context

FreshKart Mart is a supermarket chain with 42 stores in Chennai, Coimbatore, Madurai, Trichy and smaller towns. It sells groceries, dairy, bread, snacks and household items.

It makes money on a small margin (about 8 to 12 paise on every rupee) on thousands of items. So every packet sitting unsold, and every customer who walks out because a shelf is empty, directly eats into profit.

Every bill at the counter is saved in the POS system. That means the company already has years of data about what sells, where, and when. Until now, nobody was using it to decide how much to order.

📘 Key Term: POS (Point of Sale)
The billing machine and software at the checkout counter. Every time a cashier scans an item, the POS records what was sold, how many, at what price, in which store and at what time.

⚠️3The Business Problem

“Our Coimbatore store ran out of milk and bread every weekend, while Madurai had rice bags sitting for two months. We are losing sales and blocking cash at the same time.”— Senior manager, FreshKart Mart

The cost of not solving it

  • Lost sales: about ₹38 lakh a month from customers who could not find what they wanted.
  • Blocked cash: about ₹6.5 crore of extra stock lying in store backrooms.
  • Wastage: expired milk, bread and curd worth about ₹9 lakh a month thrown away.
  • Customer trust: shoppers who find empty shelves twice often switch to a quick-commerce app.
⭐ Why this matters
Inventory is the biggest use of cash in retail. Fixing it frees money the business can use to open new stores, without borrowing.

🤔4Why Data Analysis?

Gut-feeling decisions that were failing

  • Store managers ordered “the same as last month”, ignoring festivals and weekends.
  • Head office pushed equal stock to every store, even though a Chennai store and a small-town store sell very differently.
  • Nobody could see all 42 stores at once, so excess in one store was never moved to another store that was running out.

Questions the data could answer

  1. Which products run out most often, and in which stores?
  2. How many days of sales does each store's current stock cover?
  3. Do sales change by day of the week or during festivals?
  4. How much cash is locked in slow-moving stock?

🗄️5The Data

Data sources

  • POS sales data (every bill, 2 years)
  • Daily closing stock per store per product
  • Purchase orders and supplier delivery dates
  • Festival and holiday calendar

Data type: Structured, historical (2 years) plus a daily update.

Key columns

  • store_id Which store, e.g. S07
  • product Item name and pack size
  • avg_weekly_sales Average units sold in a week
  • stock_on_hand Units on the shelf + backroom today
  • stockout_days_30 Days in the last 30 when stock was zero

Data quality issues found and fixed

  • Negative stock values (e.g. −12) caused by billing errors. Fixed by setting them to zero and flagging them for the store.
  • Same product, different names: “Milk 500ML” vs “milk 500 ml”. Fixed by matching on the product barcode instead of the name.
  • Duplicate uploads: some days were uploaded twice by the POS. Removed with a duplicate check on bill number.
  • Missing store IDs in about 2% of rows. Filled using the POS terminal number.

🧭6The Analysis Journey

  1. 1🧹 Collect & Clean

    What: Pulled 2 years of sales and stock data from the database and fixed the four data quality problems above.

    Why: Wrong data gives wrong answers. Cleaning usually takes the most time.

    Tool SQL, Python (Pandas)

  2. 2🔍 Explore (EDA)

    What: Looked at sales by store, product, day of the week and festival weeks. Checked averages and spotted odd values.

    Why: To understand normal patterns before looking for problems.

    Tool Excel pivot tables, Pandas

  3. 3🧮 Analyze

    What: Calculated days of cover and stockout rate for every store-product pair. Grouped them into “too little”, “healthy” and “too much”.

    Why: These two numbers directly show understock and overstock.

    Tool Python (Pandas)

  4. 4📈 Visualize

    What: Built a dashboard with a colour-coded store map and a list of products to reorder or transfer.

    Why: Managers need to see the problem in seconds, not read a spreadsheet.

    Tool Power BI

  5. 5🎯 Find Insights

    What: Found the patterns (below) and turned each into a recommended action.

    Why: Analysis is only useful if it changes a decision.

    Tool Power BI, PowerPoint

🧠 Analyst Tip
Always start with one simple question for each step. For example, in Explore: “What does a normal week look like for milk?” Simple questions lead to clear charts.

🧮 Worked example: Days of Cover for Basmati Rice at store S12 (Madurai)
  1. Average daily sales = 70 bags per week ÷ 7 = 10 bags a day
  2. Days of cover = 600 bags ÷ 10 bags a day = 60 days
  3. The target is 14 days, so the store only needs 14 × 10 = 140 bags.
  4. Extra bags = 600 − 140 = 460 bags. At ₹450 cost per bag, that is ₹2,07,000 of cash locked in one product in one store.

Meaning: S12 should stop ordering rice and send some bags to a store that is running short.

💡9Insights and Decision

Key insights

  1. 118% of store-product pairs had more than 45 days of cover, while 11% had stockouts. Often it was the same product in different stores.

    So what? The company was not buying too much or too little overall. Stock was simply in the wrong stores. Moving stock is cheaper than buying more.

  2. 2Bread and milk had the highest stockout rates. Saturday and Sunday sales were 1.6 times the weekday sales, but deliveries were the same every day.

    So what? Perishables need day-of-week ordering. A flat daily order guarantees weekend shortages.

  3. 3Oil and rice sales jumped 2.3 times in Pongal and Diwali weeks, but orders were placed only one week before.

    So what? Festival demand is predictable. Orders must go out 3 to 4 weeks in advance.

  4. 4Stores whose managers changed orders by gut had 2× more overstock than stores that followed suggested quantities.

    So what? A simple data-based reorder rule beats guessing, and is easier to train new managers on.

✅ The decision

Management introduced an automatic weekly reorder rule: each store keeps 14 days of cover for dry items and 3 days for perishables, with higher weekend quantities for milk and bread. A store-to-store transfer list is sent every Monday, and festival orders are placed 4 weeks ahead.

🎓10Takeaways and Practice

Key lessons

  • Overstock and understock often happen together. The real problem is usually distribution, not total quantity.
  • Days of cover is the single most useful number for inventory decisions.
  • Day-of-week and festival patterns are easy to find and hugely valuable in Indian retail.
  • A good dashboard turns analysis into action that store staff can follow every day.
💭 Think Like an Analyst
  1. If a product has 2 days of cover on Friday, what will likely happen by Sunday?
  2. Why could ordering “the same as last month” fail in October and November?
  3. Which other data (weather, local events) could improve the reorder rule?
  4. Is a 0% stockout rate always good? What could it cost?

Case 2 of 10 · E-commerce

StyleBazaar: 8 out of 10 shoppers leave items in the cart

Online fashion store (app + website), Bengaluru · Beginner

📌1Snapshot Card

Domain
E-commerce
Company type
Online fashion store (app + website), Bengaluru
Analyst role
Product / Web Analyst
Difficulty
Beginner
Tools used
Google Analytics 4, SQL, Python (Pandas), Tableau
The question this case answers

Where in the checkout do shoppers give up, and why?

🏢2Business Context

StyleBazaar sells clothes, footwear and accessories online. Customers browse on the app or website, add items to a cart, and pay with UPI, cards or cash on delivery.

It earns money on every order it delivers. It also spends heavily on ads to bring visitors to the site. If visitors leave without buying, that ad money is wasted.

Every tap and click on the app is recorded as an event. This makes it possible to see exactly at which step a shopper stopped.

📘 Key Term: Funnel
The steps a customer goes through before buying: visit → view product → add to cart → checkout → pay → order. It is called a funnel because many people enter at the top and fewer reach the bottom, like water through a funnel.

⚠️3The Business Problem

“1,20,000 people added something to their cart last month, but only 24,000 bought. We are paying for traffic and losing it at the last step.”— Senior manager, StyleBazaar

The cost of not solving it

  • 96,000 abandoned carts a month. At an average order of ₹1,450, that is about ₹13.9 crore of sales not completed.
  • Wasted ad spend: each visitor costs about ₹6 to bring in through ads.
  • Lost customers: a shopper who fails to pay once is less likely to come back.
⭐ Why this matters
Fixing the last steps of a funnel is usually cheaper than buying more traffic. A small improvement at checkout multiplies across every visitor.

🤔4Why Data Analysis?

Gut-feeling decisions that were failing

  • The marketing team believed prices were too high and wanted to give bigger discounts.
  • The tech team believed the app was fine because it worked on their own phones.
  • No one had measured which step lost the most people, so every team blamed a different cause.

Questions the data could answer

  1. At which funnel step do most shoppers drop off?
  2. Is the drop different on Android, iPhone and desktop?
  3. Which payment methods fail most often?
  4. Does showing the delivery fee late make people leave?

🗄️5The Data

Data sources

  • App and website event tracking (Google Analytics 4)
  • Order database
  • Payment gateway logs (success and failure codes)
  • Customer support tickets about payment

Data type: Structured event data, near real-time, plus 6 months of history.

Key columns

  • session_id One visit by one shopper
  • device Android app, iOS app, mobile web or desktop
  • reached_step The last funnel step reached
  • payment_method UPI, card, COD
  • order_placed Yes or No
  • drop_reason Error or screen seen just before leaving

Data quality issues found and fixed

  • Bot traffic: about 4% of sessions were automated bots. Removed using session length and known bot signatures.
  • Missing events: some old app versions did not send the “payment started” event. Those sessions were excluded from the payment analysis.
  • Duplicate orders: double taps created two order records. Removed by matching order time and amount.

🧭6The Analysis Journey

  1. 1🧹 Collect & Clean

    What: Exported one month of session events and joined them with orders and payment logs. Removed bots and duplicates.

    Why: To get one clean row per visit showing how far the shopper got.

    Tool SQL (BigQuery), Pandas

  2. 2🔍 Explore (EDA)

    What: Counted shoppers at each funnel step and split by device and payment method.

    Why: To see where the biggest drop happens before guessing why.

    Tool Google Analytics 4, Excel

  3. 3🧮 Analyze

    What: Calculated step-by-step conversion and compared drop-off between groups. Read support tickets for the top drop reasons.

    Why: Numbers show where; tickets and error codes explain why.

    Tool Python (Pandas)

  4. 4📈 Visualize

    What: Built a funnel chart and a payment failure dashboard by bank and app version.

    Why: One funnel picture aligned all teams on the real problem.

    Tool Tableau

  5. 5🎯 Find Insights

    What: Ranked problems by number of lost orders and suggested fixes for each.

    Why: Fix the biggest leak first.

    Tool Tableau, Excel

🧠 Analyst Tip
Look at step-to-step conversion, not just overall conversion. A 2.4% overall rate hides which single step is broken.

📊8KPIs and Metrics

🧮 Worked example: Last month's funnel
  1. Visits 10,00,000 → Product views 6,00,000 → Add to cart 1,20,000 → Checkout 60,000 → Payment page 42,000 → Orders 24,000
  2. Conversion rate = 24,000 ÷ 10,00,000 × 100 = 2.4%
  3. Cart abandonment = (1,20,000 − 24,000) ÷ 1,20,000 × 100 = 80%
  4. Payment success = 24,000 ÷ 42,000 × 100 = 57.1% (very low)

Meaning: 18,000 people reached the payment page and still could not buy. That is the biggest single leak.

💡9Insights and Decision

Key insights

  1. 1The biggest single drop was on the payment page: 42,000 reached it but only 24,000 paid. UPI timeouts on Android caused most failures.

    So what? These shoppers had already decided to buy. Fixing payment is pure gain, with no extra ad spend.

  2. 2The ₹79 delivery fee appeared only at checkout. Sessions that saw it were twice as likely to leave.

    So what? Surprise costs feel like a trick. Show fees early or offer a free-delivery threshold.

  3. 3Mobile web users were forced to log in before checkout; 31% left at that screen.

    So what? Guest checkout removes a step that adds no value for the shopper.

  4. 4Shoppers who received a reminder within 1 hour returned 3× more than those reminded the next day.

    So what? Speed matters in recovering abandoned carts.

✅ The decision

The team fixed UPI retry on Android (auto-switch to UPI app intent), showed the delivery fee on the product page with free delivery above ₹499, added guest checkout, and sent WhatsApp cart reminders within 1 hour.

🎓10Takeaways and Practice

Key lessons

  • Break a big problem into steps. A funnel shows exactly where to act.
  • Combine numbers (where people drop) with qualitative data (support tickets) to learn why.
  • Small UX changes like guest checkout can beat big discounts.
  • Always split by device; averages hide platform-specific bugs.
💭 Think Like an Analyst
  1. Why might discounts not fix a payment timeout problem?
  2. If conversion rises but AOV falls, is the business better off? How would you check?
  3. What other events would you track on a checkout page?
  4. How would you prove the delivery-fee change caused the improvement (and not a sale season)?

Kaveri Small Finance Bank: Too many personal loans are not being repaid

Small finance bank giving personal loans, South India · Advanced

📌1Snapshot Card

Domain
Banking & Finance
Company type
Small finance bank giving personal loans, South India
Analyst role
Credit Risk Analyst
Difficulty
Advanced
Tools used
SQL, Python (Pandas, scikit-learn), Excel, Power BI
The question this case answers

Which loan applicants are likely to stop repaying, and how do we spot them before approving?

🏢2Business Context

Kaveri Small Finance Bank gives personal loans of ₹50,000 to ₹10 lakh to salaried people, small business owners and gig workers.

The bank earns interest on every loan. But when a borrower stops paying, the bank loses not just the interest but the money it lent. One bad loan can wipe out the profit from ten good ones.

Every application already has income, existing EMIs, credit score and job type. The bank also knows which past borrowers repaid and which did not. That history is what the analyst learns from.

📘 Key Term: Default
When a borrower stops paying their loan EMIs. In this case study, a loan counts as defaulted if no EMI is paid for 90 days in a row (this is also called an NPA, Non-Performing Asset).

⚠️3The Business Problem

“Our default rate on personal loans has climbed to 8.5%. Branches approve loans to meet targets, and we find out who can't pay only after six months.”— Senior manager, Kaveri Small Finance Bank

The cost of not solving it

  • ₹51 crore of loans in default out of a ₹600 crore personal loan book.
  • Regulator attention: RBI watches a bank's NPA levels closely; high NPAs limit growth.
  • Collection cost: chasing unpaid loans costs staff time and recovery agency fees.
⭐ Why this matters
Lending is the bank's core business. Better risk decisions mean it can lend more to good customers and less to risky ones, without turning everyone away.

🤔4Why Data Analysis?

Gut-feeling decisions that were failing

  • Branch managers approved loans based on how the applicant “seemed” in the meeting.
  • A single rule (credit score above 650) was used, ignoring how much EMI the person already paid.
  • Month-end targets pushed staff to approve borderline cases quickly.

Questions the data could answer

  1. Which applicant details are most linked to default?
  2. How much existing EMI is too much for a given income?
  3. Can we give each applicant a simple risk score before approval?
  4. If we tighten rules, how many good customers will we turn away?

🗄️5The Data

Data sources

  • Loan application form data
  • Credit bureau report (CIBIL score, past loans)
  • Repayment history of 12,000 past loans
  • Bank account statements (salary credits)

Data type: Structured, historical (3 years of approved loans and their outcomes).

Key columns

  • applicant_id Unique ID (no names, to protect privacy)
  • monthly_income Verified income in ₹
  • existing_emi EMIs already being paid each month
  • credit_score CIBIL score from 300 to 900
  • employment_type Salaried, self-employed, gig worker
  • defaulted Yes if 90+ days unpaid

Data quality issues found and fixed

  • Self-reported income was often higher than salary credits in the bank statement. The verified (lower) figure was used.
  • Missing credit scores for first-time borrowers (about 7%). Kept as a separate “new to credit” group instead of deleting them.
  • Imbalanced data: only 8.5% of loans defaulted, so the model had to be checked carefully, not just on overall accuracy.

🧭6The Analysis Journey

  1. 1🧹 Collect & Clean

    What: Joined application, bureau and repayment tables for 12,000 loans. Used verified income and flagged missing scores.

    Why: The model can only learn from correct, complete history.

    Tool SQL, Pandas

  2. 2🔍 Explore (EDA)

    What: Compared default rates across income bands, credit score bands, job types and EMI burden.

    Why: To find which factors actually separate good and bad borrowers.

    Tool Pandas, Excel

  3. 3🧮 Analyze

    What: Built a logistic regression model that gives each applicant a probability of default, and tested it on loans it had never seen.

    Why: A score combines many factors fairly and the same way every time.

    Tool Python (scikit-learn)

  4. 4📈 Visualize

    What: Created a risk dashboard showing default rate by score band and branch.

    Why: Credit managers must trust and understand the score before using it.

    Tool Power BI

  5. 5🎯 Find Insights

    What: Converted scores into three simple bands with a clear action for each.

    Why: Branch staff need a rule they can follow, not a formula.

    Tool Excel, Power BI

🧠 Analyst Tip
In risk problems, check what share of actual defaulters your model catches (called recall), not just overall accuracy. A model that says “nobody defaults” is 91.5% accurate here and completely useless.

🧮 Worked example: Portfolio default rate and one applicant's FOIR
  1. Past loans = 12,000. Loans that defaulted = 1,020.
  2. Default rate = 1,020 ÷ 12,000 × 100 = 8.5%
  3. Applicant A102: existing EMI ₹27,000 ÷ income ₹60,000 × 100 = 45% FOIR
  4. After a new ₹5 lakh loan (EMI about ₹11,100 at 12% interest for 5 years), FOIR becomes (27,000 + 11,100) ÷ 60,000 × 100 = 63.5%

Meaning: nearly two-thirds of A102's income would go to EMIs. That is a strong warning sign, and A102 did default.

💡9Insights and Decision

Key insights

  1. 1Applicants with FOIR above 40% defaulted at 19.8%, compared with 5.8% for those below 40%.

    So what? Existing debt matters as much as credit score. A person can have a decent score and still be over-borrowed.

  2. 2Credit score bands showed a clear staircase: below 650 defaulted at 22%, 750+ at only 2.5%.

  4. 4Loans approved in the last 3 days of a month defaulted 1.7× more.

    So what? Sales targets were pushing weak approvals. That is a process problem, not a customer problem.

🏢2Business Context

Hilltop Residency runs hotels in hill stations and beach towns. Guests book through its website and online travel apps.

It earns money from room nights. A room left empty tonight can never be sold again, so every unsold night is lost revenue forever. The same idea applies to flight seats, bus seats and even food delivery slots at peak dinner time.

Every booking records when it was made, for which date and at what price. This booking history shows how demand builds up for each date.

📘 Key Term: Dynamic pricing
Changing the price based on expected demand, like flight tickets or cab surge pricing. High demand means a higher price; low demand means a lower price to attract guests.

⚠️3The Business Problem

“Our Kodaikanal hotel sells out every summer weekend by Wednesday, at the same price as a rainy Tuesday in July. We are leaving money on the table and still have empty rooms.”— Senior manager, Hilltop Residency Hotels

The cost of not solving it

  • Underpriced peak dates: rooms sold out early at normal prices, with many guests turned away.
  • Empty weekdays: occupancy of 30 to 40% in monsoon months.
  • About ₹3.5 crore a year of revenue opportunity estimated across 12 hotels.
⭐ Why this matters
In hospitality, the right price on the right date is the difference between profit and loss. Rooms are perishable, like vegetables.

🤔4Why Data Analysis?

Gut-feeling decisions that were failing

  • Prices were set once a year as “season” and “off-season” rates.
  • Hotel managers raised prices only after rooms were nearly full, too late to matter.
  • Nobody tracked how fast bookings came in for each date.

Questions the data could answer

  1. How many rooms will each hotel sell on each date?
  2. Which dates sell out early, and how early?
  3. How do school holidays, festivals and weather change demand?
  4. What price gives the most revenue, not just the most bookings?

Data quality issues found and fixed

  • Cancellations were still counted as bookings in some reports. Removed cancelled bookings for actual demand.
  • Group bookings (weddings, company trips) caused big one-off spikes. Separated from regular demand.

🧭6The Analysis Journey

  1. 1🧹 Collect & Clean

    What: Collected 3 years of bookings, removed cancellations and separated group bookings.

    Why: The forecast must learn from real, regular demand.

    Tool SQL, Pandas

  2. 2🔍 Explore (EDA)

    What: Plotted occupancy by day of week, month, holiday and weather; tracked how bookings build up before each date.

    Why: To learn the seasonal patterns and booking pace.

    Tool Pandas, Excel

  3. 3🧮 Analyze

    What: Built a time-series forecast of rooms sold for each hotel and date for the next 90 days, and tested it on past months.

    Why: A reliable forecast is the base for setting prices.

    Tool Python (time-series model)

  4. 4📈 Visualize

    What: Created a daily pricing dashboard showing forecast occupancy, current bookings and suggested price.

    Why: Revenue managers decide prices every morning.

    Tool Power BI

  5. 5🎯 Find Insights

    What: Set simple price rules tied to forecast occupancy.

    Why: Rules keep pricing consistent across 12 hotels.

    Tool Excel, Power BI

🧠 Analyst Tip
For forecasting, always test on a period the model has not seen (for example, train on 2 years, test on the 3rd). A forecast that only fits the past is not useful.

MetricFormulaWhat it means in plain wordsGood or bad signal

🧮 Worked example: RevPAR for Ooty on Fri 12 April
  1. Room revenue = 76 rooms × ₹4,800 = ₹3,64,800
  2. Occupancy = 76 ÷ 80 × 100 = 95%
  3. RevPAR = ₹3,64,800 ÷ 80 rooms = ₹4,560 (same as 95% × ₹4,800)
  4. Compare Tue 16 April: 34 × ₹3,200 = ₹1,08,800 ÷ 80 = ₹1,360 RevPAR

Meaning: a long-weekend Friday earns over 3 times more per room than a normal Tuesday. Prices should reflect that gap early, not at the last minute.

💡9Insights and Decision

Key insights

  1. 1Peak weekends and long weekends sold out 2 to 3 weeks early at the normal price, and 25+ guests were turned away per date.

    So what? Demand was much higher than supply. Prices could rise without losing guests.

  2. 2Monsoon weekdays averaged 30 to 40% occupancy at full price.

    So what? A lower weekday price or work-from-hills packages can fill rooms that would earn nothing.

  3. 3Bookings in hand 30 days before a date predicted final occupancy within about 8.5% error.

    So what? Booking pace gives an early, reliable signal for setting prices.

  4. 4School holiday calendars from three states explained most summer peaks.

    So what? Free public calendars are powerful forecast inputs.

✅ The decision

Hilltop introduced forecast-based price bands: forecast below 50% occupancy → 15% lower price; 50–80% → base price; 80–95% → 15% higher; above 95% → 25% higher (with a fixed upper limit). It also launched weekday work-from-hills packages for the monsoon season.

🎓10Takeaways and Practice

Key lessons

  • When products are perishable (rooms, seats, delivery slots), price and demand must be managed together.
  • Forecasting comes first; pricing decisions depend on it.
  • RevPAR balances price and occupancy, so it is better than either alone.
  • Simple, rule-based pricing is easier to trust than a black-box price.

  4. What would you do when the forecast is wrong for a sudden event?

5 common patterns across industries

  1. 1
    Start with a business question, not a tool

    Every case began with a manager's problem in plain words. Tools came later and changed from case to case.

  2. 2
    Cleaning the data is always the first real job

    All 10 cases had missing values, duplicates or inconsistent names. Clean data is the foundation of every correct answer.

  3. 3
    A few causes create most of the problem

    Two diseases, three downtime reasons, two teams, one hub: focusing on the biggest drivers gave the biggest wins.

  4. 4
    Averages hide the real story; always split the data

    Splitting by store, device, team, shift or day of week revealed problems that the overall average hid.

  5. 5
    Insight is only valuable when it changes a decision

    Every case ended with a clear action and measured expected versus actual impact. That is what makes an analyst valuable.
